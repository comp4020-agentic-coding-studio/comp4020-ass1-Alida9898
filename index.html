How the owl does it

Two ears give you two facts about a sound.

Which ear hears it first. Sound takes time to travel, so the nearer ear hears it a fraction sooner. That head start is a direction: left, or right.

Which ear hears it louder. An ear is not equally sensitive in every direction — it hears best along the direction it points. So which ear is louder depends on where each one is aimed.

And that is where you and an owl part company.

Look at the two pairs of cones. The owl's point at different things, so its two facts are two facts. Yours are mirror images, so your second fact is a copy of your first — and a copy is not a second measurement. That is why louder can tell an owl a height and cannot tell you one.

Then why not just tilt your head?

A fair objection, and the first one most people reach for. Tilt your head twenty-five degrees and one ear really is higher than the other — the same twenty-five degrees a barn owl has. So what is different?

What moved. A tilt takes your ear positions round with it, and your ear positions are what set the timing axis. So both cues turn together and stay parallel: timing and loudness go on answering the same question, just a question rotated twenty-five degrees off horizontal.

An owl's offset moves only where each ear listens hardest. Its ears stay on opposite sides of its head, so the timing stays horizontal while the loudness swings vertical. Same angle — one thing moved instead of two.

Two rulers taped in parallel measure one direction, however you turn the pair. Tilting turns the pair. An owl's skull changes the angle between them.

That difference has a price you can count. Tilted twenty-five degrees, there are pairs of places nearly ten target widths apart that reach your ears with exactly the same timing and exactly the same loudness — no way to tell them apart, from either cue. Hand the same twenty-five degrees to an owl and it hears those two places differently.

What does work is tilting and listening again. That is a second reading from a new angle: one instrument, used twice. It is genuinely how you place a sound above your head, and it is why you turn towards a noise without deciding to. But it costs a second moment — and it needs the sound to still be there when you take it. A mouse under snow rustles once, for a fraction of a second, and does not oblige you with a repeat.

That is what the lopsided skull buys: it turns a two-step procedure into one step. Barn owls do bob and swivel their heads, at length, when they have time to. The asymmetry is for when they do not.

Why so lopsided?

It is not enough for the ears to be uneven. Working a height out of the loudness difference means dividing by how much that difference moves per unit of height — and that divisor shrinks with the offset. So a nearly level pair takes a small misreading and turns it into a wild answer. Slide the offset down and watch what a single decibel of slop costs.

Which is why a barn owl is not slightly lopsided. Evolution did not stop at two degrees, because two degrees is not a measurement. (The hunt above is noiseless — real ears are not, and height is the coarser of an owl's two axes even at full offset.)

If you pressed Listen, you have already run the experiment on yourself. The timing difference arrived as a direction, unmistakably, without any effort on your part. The loudness difference arrived as a lean to one side — because it was played into your ears, and a level pair has nowhere else to put it. An owl's height cue, delivered to human ears, comes out as left and right. You cannot hear it as height. That is not the simulation falling short; it is the whole argument, in a form you can check without reading another word.

What just happened to you

With an owl's ears, two facts and two axes give one place. That is not a knack you picked up — it is arithmetic the shape of its skull does for it, before it has moved a muscle.

Then the second fact folded back into the first. Notice what did not happen: the ears did not go quiet. They still report a difference in loudness, and it is still a real measurement — the number stayed on screen the whole time. What broke is the step after it. Aim both ears the same way and the loudness difference stops varying with height at all, so there is no height for it to be pointing at. Solve for one and the term is gone.

So the second half was not a worse instrument. It was one instrument fewer — and it was you. Those are your ears. You have had them your whole life, you have never once placed a sound by height by comparing them, and until you missed a mouse in the dark you had no reason to notice.

Look at the marks on the field. Under your own ears the green ones sit in a band at whatever height you kept guessing, and the red ones fill in everywhere else. Left and right were perfect all along, which is why every miss was vertical.

Aiming an ear is what turns it into a measuring instrument along that axis. Two ears pointed the same way are one instrument used twice; point them apart and the pair answers a question neither could answer alone. A barn owl's skull is lopsided for exactly one reason, and it is the reason it eats.

The owl's uneven ears

An owl's ears are uneven. Yours are not.

Owls hunt in the dark better than almost anything else that flies, and everyone knows the trick is hearing rather than eyes. But you have two ears as well. So what is an owl's hearing doing that yours is not?

Below is a mouse you cannot see, and the two instruments an owl finds one with. The gauge along the bottom of the field says how far across it is; the gauge up the side says how high. Put your aim where they cross, and strike. Why that works is further down — try it first.

Those are gone. You now have your own ears: level with each other, like every human's. Nothing else has changed — same dark, same mouse, same left-to-right timing.

How the owl does it

Two ears give you two facts about a sound.

Which ear hears it first. Sound takes time to travel, so the nearer ear hears it a fraction sooner. That head start is a direction: left, or right.

Which ear hears it louder. An ear is not equally sensitive in every direction — it hears best along the direction it points. So which ear is louder depends on where each one is aimed.

And that is where you and an owl part company.

Look at the two pairs of cones. The owl's point at different things, so its two facts are two facts. Yours are mirror images, so your second fact is a copy of your first — and a copy is not a second measurement. That is why louder can tell an owl a height and cannot tell you one.

Then why not just tilt your head?

Tilt twenty-five degrees and one ear really is higher than the other — the same twenty-five degrees a barn owl has. The difference is what moved. A tilt carries your ear positions round with it, and positions are what set the timing axis, so both cues turn together and stay parallel. An owl's offset moves only where each ear listens hardest. Its ears stay on opposite sides of its head, so timing stays horizontal while loudness swings vertical.

Two rulers taped in parallel measure one direction, however you turn the pair. Tilting turns the pair. An owl's skull changes the angle between them.

Tilted twenty-five degrees, there are places nearly ten target widths apart that reach your ears with identical timing and identical loudness. An owl at the same twenty-five degrees hears them apart. Tilting and listening again does work — one instrument used twice — but it costs a second moment, and a mouse under snow rustles once.

Why so lopsided?

Working a height out of the loudness difference means dividing by how much that difference moves per unit of height — and that divisor shrinks with the offset, so a nearly level pair turns a small misreading into a wild answer. Slide it down and watch what one decibel of slop costs.

If you pressed Listen, you already ran this on yourself. The timing arrived as a direction without any effort. The loudness arrived as a lean to one side — because it was played into your ears, and a level pair has nowhere else to put it. An owl's height cue, delivered to human ears, comes out as left and right.

What just happened to you

Notice what did not happen when the ears were levelled: they did not go quiet. They still reported a difference in loudness, and it was still a real measurement — the number stayed on screen the whole time. What broke was the step after it. There was no height for it to be pointing at.

So the second half was not a worse instrument. It was one instrument fewer — and it was you. Those are your ears. You have had them your whole life, you have never once placed a sound by height by comparing them, and until you missed a mouse in the dark you had no reason to notice. Look at the marks: your green ones sit in a band at whatever height you kept guessing. Left and right were perfect all along, which is why every miss was vertical.

Aiming an ear is what turns it into a measuring instrument along that axis. Two ears pointed the same way are one instrument used twice. A barn owl's skull is lopsided for exactly one reason, and it is the reason it eats.

How the owl does it

Two ears give you two facts about a sound.

Which hears it first. The nearer ear wins by a fraction of a millisecond, and that head start is a direction: left, or right. Everything with two ears has this one.

Which hears it louder. An ear hears best along the way it points, so louder depends on where each one is aimed — and this is where the owl parts company with the rest of us.

The owl's cones point at different things, so its two facts are genuinely two. Yours are mirror images: the second only repeats the first, and a repeat is not a measurement.

If you pressed Listen, you ran this on yourself. The timing arrived as a direction, effortlessly. The loudness arrived as a lean to one side — a level pair has nowhere else to put it.

Two fair objections

Then why not just tilt your head?

Tilt twenty-five degrees and one ear really is higher than the other — the same twenty-five degrees an owl has. But look at what moved. A tilt carries the ear positions round with it, and positions set the timing axis, so both cues turn together and stay parallel. The owl moves only where each ear listens hardest. Timing stays horizontal; loudness swings vertical.

Two rulers taped in parallel measure one direction, however you turn the pair. Tilting turns the pair. An owl's skull changes the angle between them.

Not that tilting is useless — you can tell high from low, just not this way. The folds of your outer ear stamp a sound with the angle it came from, and you can always turn your head and listen twice.

Both are shut to an owl. Feathers build directionality — that is the facial ruff — but not that fine filtering. And listening twice costs time it has not got: one rustle, then a dive at speed through its own wind noise, prey still moving, no chance to stop and re-aim.

The asymmetry is not a better answer to the same problem. It is the only answer left to a harder one.

And why lopsided by so much?

Working a height out of the loudness difference means dividing by how much that difference moves per unit of height — and that divisor shrinks with the offset. Nearly level, one decibel of slop becomes a wild answer. Slide it down and watch.

And what about you?

The levelled ears in the hunt were not a thought experiment. They were yours — you have had them your whole life, and you have never once placed a sound by height by comparing them.

Look at the marks on the field. The green sit in a band at whatever height you kept guessing; the red fill in everywhere else. Left and right were perfect all along. Every miss was vertical.

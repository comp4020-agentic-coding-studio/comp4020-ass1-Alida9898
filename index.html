The owl's uneven ears

An owl's ears are uneven. That is the whole trick.

A barn owl drops onto a mouse it has never seen — in the dark, under snow. Not because its eyes are extraordinary. Because its two ear openings point in different directions: the left one aimed down, the right one aimed up.

Almost every animal with two ears can tell left from right. Sound reaches the near ear first, and the gap between the two arrivals is the direction. But that gap says nothing about height — every point directly above another gives an identical reading. Uneven ears are how the owl gets the second axis, and they are why it can hit something it cannot see.

So: you are the owl. It is dark. You have two cues and nothing else.

What just happened

Level the ears and the loudness meter keeps moving. It still leans left and right, because the ears are still on opposite sides of the head. What it no longer does is tell you anything about height — the reading at the top of the field is identical to the reading at the bottom. Not weaker. Identical.

That is the part worth taking away. The cue did not get noisier, and nothing looked broken. A meter that moves is not the same as a meter that knows. You were guessing the vertical the whole time, and the scoreboard is where you found out.

Aiming an ear is what makes it a measuring instrument. Two ears aimed the same way are one instrument used twice; aim them apart and the pair answers a question neither could answer alone. The owl's skull is asymmetric for exactly one reason, and it is the reason it eats.

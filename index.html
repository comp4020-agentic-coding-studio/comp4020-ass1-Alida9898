The owl's uneven ears

An owl's ears are uneven. That is the whole trick.

A barn owl drops onto a mouse it has never seen — in the dark, under snow. Not because its eyes are extraordinary. Because its two ear openings point in different directions: the left one aimed down, the right one aimed up.

Almost every animal with two ears can tell left from right. Sound reaches the nearer ear a fraction sooner, and that head start is the direction. But it says nothing about height — a mouse above you and a mouse below you arrive at both ears alike. Uneven ears are how the owl gets the second axis, and they are why it can hit what it cannot see.

An owl does not grope around for its target. It hears the mouse once, works out a direction, and drops. So you get the same two instruments it has: one along the bottom of the field, reading off how far across the sound is, and one up the side, reading off how high. Put your aim where the two cross.

What just happened

With uneven ears, two readings and two axes give one place. That is not a knack you picked up — it is arithmetic the shape of the owl's head does for it, before it has moved a muscle. The gauge up the side was a height, the same way a thermometer is a temperature.

Level the ears and that gauge has nothing to put on it. Notice what did not happen: the ears did not go quiet. They still report a difference in loudness, and it is still a real measurement — the number is right there. What broke is the step after it. Aim both ears the same way and the loudness difference stops varying with height at all, so there is no height it could be pointing at. Solve for it and the term is gone.

So you were not working with a worse instrument. You were working with one fewer instrument, and the only way to find that out was to strike and miss. Left and right stayed perfect the whole time, which is why every miss was vertical, and why you still connected now and then — you knew the column, and the rest was luck.

Aiming an ear is what turns it into a measuring instrument along that axis. Two ears pointed the same way are one instrument used twice; point them apart and the pair answers a question neither could answer alone. The owl's skull is lopsided for exactly one reason, and it is the reason it eats.
